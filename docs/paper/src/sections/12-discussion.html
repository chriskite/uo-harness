Discussion

What we would tell someone starting this tomorrow, where the evidence is thin, and the parts that are not purely technical.

Lessons

1. Put the LLM above the loop, not in it

Every attempt to let a language model act inside a reflex loop ended badly. The hand-fought dungeon hunt that killed Shackleworth in about fifteen seconds produced the rule we now quote most: "An LLM is way too slow to react for combat" (). The design that worked gives the model a database of junctures, a small CLI and a wake-up call, and leaves chopping, walking and fleeing to code with typed failures (). The model's best contributions were judgement calls no controller could make: abandoning an unworkable grove, noticing a pattern across shifts, writing up what went wrong.

2. A shared store is the cheapest integration layer

Putting every event, trip, chat line and fact into one SQLite file gave us a message bus, an analytics warehouse, a training set for the planner and the paper's own results chapter for the price of one schema (). The costs were real: a store of nearly 300 MB (179 MB when it was handed between computers like a baton), a visualizer whose slow LAN reader fell further and further behind (147 s after 200 s in a replay with a reader throttled to 1.5 MB/s) until its state updates were trimmed, and constant care about who is allowed to write.

3. Model uncertainty, then act on samples of it

The planner never needed to be right about any one spot. Thompson sampling plus honest posteriors (and a prior that says how many logs a grove can hold) produced "almost always exploring" behaviour at the start, which the owner insisted was exactly right for a Bayesian process with 132 candidate spots, of which only 26 had a trip by 10-05 (). The same discipline caught real mistakes: aborted trips that had been silently flattering dangerous spots, and a board price assumed at 9.5 gp that the market put at 26.8.

4. The field report loop is the product

The most valuable feedback came from the Seer playing live and posting what hurt: a runner idling next to an air dragon, a door that ate a step, a landing rune eight tiles off. A coding agent in the main session fixed it, a test pinned it, the next shift ran on the fix. Over the last two days that loop closed in well under an hour per issue. We think this, more than any single algorithm, is why a nine-day project reached 3,000 logs per trip-hour on its best trip ().

5. Write it down, or it did not happen

Rule 0 (record and push engineering knowledge before a session ends) turned out to be the project's real memory. Sessions ended, models changed, two computers took turns, and nothing was lost because every finding was in docs/NOTES.md or docs/PLAN.md. Its price is a 210 KB notes file that touched 46% of all commits; its payoff is that a fresh agent on a fresh machine rebuilt the working stack in about half an hour ().

Working with the agents

The owner set the goals and made the product and risk calls; the agents worked out most implementation designs within them inferred. We have no tally of decisions by origin; the record at afbbf71 holds 29 owner-decision markers in docs/NOTES.md and 19 in commit messages (counting "user decision" and "(user, …)"-style attributions), against a far larger body of unattributed design. Prompts were short (median 90 characters), and the owner's most useful moves were interrupting, asking "can you measure that?", and supplying game knowledge the agents could not look up ("thieves look friendly to us until they steal"). Two of the biggest wins started as the owner's hunches: moving the overseer from a bespoke daemon to an omp session talking to a CLI, and making subagent play the standing practice (10-03) so the main session could keep engineering (an agent had first used an overseer subagent on 09-30).

The agents' characteristic failure was confident wrongness that propagated into the docs: a day of conclusions built on a mis-decoded stream, a "gate" that was actually the client's own state, a claim of "movement solved" that a quick look at the screen contradicted. The cure was cultural, not technical: evidence-first prose, [INFERENCE] labels, and an owner who kept asking for a capture. We also recorded a milder pattern: one agent asked for informed acknowledgment of the ban risk; two others hesitated in their thinking and were interrupted; the owner then re-sent the same instruction, unchanged, to a fresh session in a new worktree, which complied ().

Limitations

  • One game, one skill, one week. Everything measured is lumberjacking plus a little hunting. The planner's structure should transfer to mining or fishing; we have not tried.
  • Small samples. Sixty-three trips over three characters, eighteen of the 26 planner spots with a trip having just one (27 counting Shelter Island, which the planner excludes), three PK deaths, all to one player (Bastet), plus seven other hostile players escaped by a clean recall. Rates like the 0.046 creature deaths per field hour will move: that is the planner's posterior (prior 0.01/h over 20 pseudo-hours) and counts only one of Dan's three 10-05 deaths; raw, it is 1 attributed creature death in 7.2 field hours.
  • Confounded progress. Throughput rose from about 355 logs per trip-hour (a three-trip Shelter Island run on 09-29) to over 3,000 on the best single trip of 10-05 (2,589 for that day as a whole), but the rise mixes a faster chop cycle, a better success rate, a different character, a different venue and Harvest Aspect armor. decomposes what it can; it is not a controlled experiment.
  • Notional costs. The $1,559 of model spend in is list price for an owner on subscription plans.
  • A moving target. Outlands patches its client and rules. During the project, what changed was mostly our understanding of its mechanics: the stationary-harvest penalty (a 2025 rule) was decoded on 10-03, and Smart Harvest, a long-standing feature, was adopted on 10-04. Every rate here is "that week".

Ethics and the shard's rules

This project automates play on a shard whose published rules prohibit unsanctioned automation. We did it knowingly, as a personal engineering project on the owner's own accounts, and we said so in the repo. The early constraints (Test Shard only, a human solves every CAPTCHA) were relaxed by the owner on 09-30, and the agents' hesitation about that is part of the record (). Several things stayed fixed throughout: the harness never attacks other players, never speaks about itself in game, never touches the client or its files, and treats a staff member in view as a reason to stop. Whether any of that makes it acceptable to the shard's community is a separate question, and the answer from the shard's own rules is no. The owner bears that risk, which is one reason this document is a private record and not a recipe.

There is also a small ecosystem point. A harvest bot competes for the same groves as human lumberjacks, and the planner's explicit objective is to out-earn them. The harness's rule that it never fights players protects the other characters; it does not make the competition fair.

Conclusion

In nine days a person and a handful of AI coding agents built a system that played a twenty-nine-year-old MMO for 26 hours of agent-driven time, chopped 18,757 logs, answered every CAPTCHA, and improved its own choices from its own records. The parts that carried the weight were unglamorous: a proxy that forwards almost everything unchanged, a world model that forgets what the client forgets, a database every component can write to, a planner that admits it does not know yet, and an LLM that is allowed to be slow because nothing urgent depends on it. The agents wrote most of the code and, we believe, worked out most of the implementation designs; the owner decided what mattered and what not to do. The next thing to learn is how much of that division of labour survives when the problems are harder than chopping wood.
