The planner's Bayesian model, step by step

This appendix spells out the statistics behind : what each symbol means, why each distribution was chosen, how evidence updates it, and how the planner turns posteriors into one runner command. It ends with one spot worked through by hand at the snapshot. Everything here describes harness/lumber_opt.py at ee59524; the numbers come from the same read-only planner run as .

Notation

Gamma distributions are written with shape and rate, \text{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta), so the mean is \alpha/\beta and the variance \alpha/\beta^2. Every rate is per field hour: time in the grove from the first tree to leaving, walking between trees included, travel lockout excluded. Weighted sums run over a spot's trips with the recency weights w_i, and a subscript w marks a weighted count (for example \text{died}_w is the sum of the weights of the spot's deaths).

Counts, rates and conjugate updates

Logs arrive while the character stands in a grove, so the natural model for a trip's haul is a count in an exposure time: L\mid\lambda\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda H). The Gamma distribution is the conjugate prior for a Poisson rate : if \lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_0,\beta_0) before a trip, it is Gamma again after it, and updating is addition.

\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_0,\beta_0),\quad L\mid\lambda\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda H)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda\mid L\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_0+L,\ \beta_0+H)

That makes \alpha_0 a pseudo-count of logs and \beta_0 a pseudo-exposure in hours. The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the spot's own rate, with weights in proportion to exposure. This is the "shrinkage" that keeps a single lucky trip from crowning a spot:

\E[\lambda\mid L]=\frac{\alpha_0+L}{\beta_0+H}=\frac{\beta_0}{\beta_0+H}\,\mu+\frac{H}{\beta_0+H}\cdot\frac{L}{H}

The same pattern repeats for every quantity the planner learns. Rates of events (sightings, deaths, early returns, thefts) are Gamma posteriors over field hours. Proportions (P(death | sighting), the yielding share of trees, the share a thief takes) are Beta posteriors, the conjugate prior of a binomial proportion, where \text{Beta}(a,b) reads as a pseudo-successes and b pseudo-failures. Conjugacy keeps the model to arithmetic with no sampler to tune, and every posterior can be drawn from in one call.

Overdispersion: why a trip counts less than its logs

Logs are not single events. A successful chop yields a lump of about eight (32,676 logs from 4,125 successes at the snapshot), so a trip's haul is a compound Poisson count: N successes at rate \nu, each yielding Y_j logs. Its variance is larger than its mean by a fixed factor:

\E[L]=\nu H\,\E[Y],\qquad \operatorname{Var}[L]=\nu H\,\E[Y^2]\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\operatorname{Var}[L]}{\E[L]}=\E[Y]+\frac{\operatorname{Var}[Y]}{\E[Y]}\ \ge\ \E[Y]\approx 8

A plain Poisson update would treat 4,000 logs as 4,000 independent events and become far too sure of itself. The planner uses the quasi-Poisson fix from generalized linear models : assume \operatorname{Var}[L]=\phi\,\E[L], estimate \phi from the trips, and divide both the count and the exposure by it. L/\phi then has mean and variance both equal to \lambda H/\phi, like a Poisson count in exposure H/\phi, and the conjugate update applies to it unchanged; that is . The estimate is Pearson's statistic over trips of at least three minutes, each against its own spot's rate:

\hat\phi=\max\Big(8,\ \frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{s}\sum_{i\in s}\frac{(L_i-\hat\lambda_s H_i)^2}{\hat\lambda_s H_i}\Big),\qquad \hat\lambda_s=\frac{\sum_{i\in s}L_i}{\sum_{i\in s}H_i}

with n trips over k spots that have at least two of them. The floor of 8 is the compound-Poisson minimum above. The measured 17.0 says trips at one spot also differ for other reasons (other lumberjacks, interruptions, different stands). In practice an hour of chopping at about 4,000 logs counts as about 235 independent events, so the rate's coefficient of variation after one field hour is about 1/\sqrt{235}\approx 6.5\%.

Recency weights

Each trip's likelihood is raised to the power w_i=0.5^{\text{age}_i/14\,\text{d}} (). In a conjugate model that simply multiplies the trip's counts and exposure by w_i, which is why every sum in the planner is weighted. This is the idea of a power prior : old data is not deleted, it is discounted, so a two-week-old trip counts half and a four-week-old one a quarter. The prior itself is not discounted, so a spot nobody has visited for months drifts back towards "a spot like the others". The half-life is a design choice, not an estimate: it encodes the belief that competition, killers and patches change on that time scale.

The empirical-Bayes prior

The prior for an unvisited spot is learned from the visited ones . Take the weighted rate \hat\lambda_s of each of the m spots with at least half a weighted field hour, use their mean \mu and sample variance, floor the spread at 35% of the mean, and choose the Gamma with that mean and variance (moment matching):

s_0^2=\max\Big(\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{s}\big(\hat\lambda_s-\mu\big)^2,\ (0.35\,\mu)^2\Big),\qquad \alpha_0=\frac{\mu^2}{s_0^2},\qquad \beta_0=\frac{\mu}{s_0^2}

At the snapshot the floor binds: \mu=3{,}166 logs per field hour, \alpha_0=1/0.35^2=8.16, \beta_0=0.00258. Because the prior enters the \phi-scaled update, its weight in real field time is \phi\beta_0=0.044 h: after about 2.6 minutes of chopping, a spot's own data carries as much weight as the prior, and a typical first trip outweighs it several times over. Until then the spot is drawn from the full spread of the measured spots, which is what makes unvisited spots attractive to the sampler. "Empirical" means the prior is estimated from the same store it is applied to, so the uncertainty in \mu itself is ignored. Only m=7 spots had the half weighted field hour needed to shape \mu at the snapshot, so that is a real approximation.

Hazards: competing clocks with partial pooling

Deaths, early returns and thefts are modelled as independent Poisson processes that run only during field time. Each has a per-spot Gamma posterior whose prior is centred on a rate pooled over every trip:

\bar h=\frac{h_0 n_0+\sum_{\text{all }i}w_i e_i}{n_0+\sum_{\text{all }i}w_iF_i},\qquad h_s\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(S\,\bar h+\sum_{i\in s}w_ie_i,\ \ S+E_s\Big)

Here e_i counts the trip's events, h_0 and n_0 are the global prior rate and its weight in pseudo-hours, and S is how many field hours of evidence the pooled rate counts for at one spot. Rare events lean hard on the pool: thefts use S=20 h and deaths 10 h, while early returns, which are common, use only 2 h. This is partial pooling, the middle ground between one rate for all spots and an independent rate per spot. Strictly, it is a two-stage empirical-Bayes shortcut: the pooled means are plugged in as known, not given their own posterior.

The death rate gets a structured prior. If hostile players appear at rate \sigma_s and each appearance kills independently with probability \pi, fatal encounters form a Poisson process of rate \sigma_s\pi (thinning); creature deaths arrive independently at h_C and simply add (superposition):

m_0=\sigma_s\,\pi\,[\text{pvp}]+h_C,\qquad \pi\sim\text{Beta}(1+pk_w,\ 3+\text{seen}_w-pk_w)

The Beta(1, 3) prior on \pi says "one death in four sightings" with the weight of four sightings. By the snapshot the weighted evidence (about 30 weighted sightings, 3.5 weighted PK deaths) had moved it to Beta(4.53, 29.78), mean 0.132. Because sightings are far more common than deaths, a spot with many sightings but no deaths yet still looks dangerous. The cost is that each PK death counts twice, once in \pi and once in its own spot's count, which flags.

Thompson sampling and P(best)

The planner never maximizes a posterior mean directly when it explores. It samples a whole world from the posterior, finds the best spot in that world, and repeats . One draw for one eligible spot is:

  1. \lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha_s,\beta_s) and T\sim\mathcal N(\bar T_s,\ \text{sd}_s), floored at 10 s;
  2. \sigma\sim\text{Gamma}(\cdot) (PvP spots only) and \pi\sim\text{Beta}(\cdot), then h_D\sim\text{Gamma}(10\,m_0(\sigma,\pi)+\text{died}_w,\ 10+E_s): a hierarchical draw, the prior's parameters first, the rate second;
  3. h_S and h_T from their Gamma posteriors, and \psi\sim\text{Beta}(\cdot), which fixes the grove's capacity;
  4. the value of the draw: \max_Q\text{Rate}(Q) under those parameters, over every other point of the trip-size grid, with Q capped by the capacity.

The creature rate h_C and the theft share f are not drawn; they enter at their pooled means. Note what is random: the draw samples parameters (what we do not know), while the renewal-reward formula below averages over the outcomes of a trip (what chance will do) in closed form. Each draw is therefore an expected rate in one plausible world.

\Pr(\text{best}=s)=\Pr_{\theta\sim p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)}\Big[s=\arg\max_{s'}\ \max_Q\ \text{Rate}_{s'}(Q;\theta_{s'})\Big]\ \approx\ \frac{1}{2000}\sum_{r=1}^{2000}\mathbb 1\big[s\text{ wins draw }r\big]

The Monte Carlo standard error is \sqrt{p(1-p)/2000}, about 0.004 at the largest P(best) of 0.030. The pick itself is the winner of one more, fresh draw, so each spot is chosen with probability equal to its P(best): Thompson sampling is probability matching. The greedy spot is the one with the highest value at the posterior means, computed with the refined trip size. The pick is labelled explore when it differs from the greedy spot.

shows why P(best) was close to uniform at the snapshot. witcher_23 is well measured, and its draws stay between 2,585 and 3,273 logs/trip-h (10th to 90th percentile). Each of the 94 eligible spots nobody had visited draws from the wide prior, so in every round some of them land far above that, and the best of 123 draws has a median of 4,710. The sampler is not claiming that unvisited spots are better on average (witcher_279's median draw is 2,479); it is saying that one of them might be, and that the only way to find out is to go. That is also why the greedy pick and P(best) can disagree so sharply, and why the overseer could choose the greedy pick once the owner's goal was boards rather than information ().

Renewal-reward with competing hazards

A trip is one cycle of a renewal process: overhead, field time until the quota or a hazard ends it, and a recovery delay after a death. By the renewal-reward theorem the long-run rate is the expected reward of a cycle over its expected length, a ratio of expectations rather than the expectation of a ratio, so it is enough to compute two expectations per trip size:

\text{Rate}(Q)=\frac{B(Q)-\text{supplies}-G\,P_D}{\E[\text{cycle time}]},\qquad \E[\text{cycle time}]=T+E_1(h,t_f)+R\,P_D

The trip ends at the first of three times: the quota time t_f=Q/\lambda, a death clock \tau_D\sim\text{Exp}(h_D), or a sent-home clock \tau_S\sim\text{Exp}(h_S). The first of two independent exponential clocks is exponential with rate h=h_D+h_S, and it is a death with probability h_D/h. So the trip survives to t with probability e^{-ht}, and:

\begin{gathered}E_1(h,x)=\int_0^{x}e^{-ht}\,dt=\frac{1-e^{-hx}}{h},\qquad \E[\text{field time}]=E_1(h,t_f),\\ P_D=h_D\,E_1(h,t_f),\qquad P_S=h_S\,E_1(h,t_f)\end{gathered}

Since P_D+P_S+e^{-ht_f}=1, no trip size can produce a death probability above 1, which is the bug the first, square-root sizing rule had on long trips. Thefts do not end the trip; each removes the share f of what is carried. Because they arrive at rate h_T independently of the load, the expected load obeys a linear equation:

\frac{dC}{dt}=\lambda-h_Tf\,C=\lambda-kC\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad C(t)=\frac{\lambda\,(1-e^{-kt})}{k}

The logs stored are the load at the quota time if the trip survives that long, or the load at the moment it is sent home. A death stores nothing:

B(Q)=e^{-ht_f}\,C(t_f)+h_S\int_0^{t_f}e^{-ht}C(t)\,dt=e^{-ht_f}\,C(t_f)+\frac{h_S\lambda}{k}\Big[E_1(h,t_f)-E_1(h+k,t_f)\Big]

As k\to 0 (no thefts) C(t)=\lambda t and the integral becomes \lambda E_2(h,t_f) with E_2(h,x)=\int_0^x te^{-ht}dt=(1-e^{-hx}-hxe^{-hx})/h^2; the code switches to that form, and to series expansions for tiny hx, to avoid cancellation (_e1, _e2). With every term in closed form, the planner evaluates Rate on a 41-point log-spaced grid of trip sizes from 200 to 10,000 logs, then refines the best one by golden-section search to 10 logs. The rate is flat near its maximum, which is why the Thompson draws can use every other grid point.

The spot's own evidence dominates the rate (96% of the weight) after only 1.1 weighted field hours, but its capacity estimate is almost pure prior, so the number that binds the trip is the least informed one. What is not fully Bayesian

  • Plug-in hyperparameters. The pooled hazard rates, \mu, s_0 and \phi are point estimates; only \pi among the pooled quantities is drawn. A full hierarchical model would widen every spot's draws a little.
  • Fixed means inside the draws. h_C and f enter every draw at their pooled means, and the regrowth window is an isotonic point fit (), not a posterior.
  • Independence. A spot's rate, overhead and hazards are drawn independently, though a crowded grove is plausibly both slower and more dangerous. Hazards accrue only in field time, so a death at a landing is counted against the spot but adds no exposure.
  • Deterministic corrections. The skill rescaling () trusts the wiki's success formula exactly, and the overhead posterior is a weighted Normal with a 30 s floor on the spread rather than a conjugate Normal model.
  • Double use of PK deaths in \pi and in the spot's count, as noted above.

We have not measured how much each simplification matters. The uncertainty that shapes the plan at the snapshot is a different one: 94 of the 123 eligible spots had never been tried ().
