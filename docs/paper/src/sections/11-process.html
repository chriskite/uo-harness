Building it with AI agents

One person wrote 545 prompts over nine evenings and daytime check-ins; omp coding agents turned them into 348 commits. This chapter looks at how that worked: the scale of the conversation, which models did the work, how work was delegated, the rules the agents were held to, and where they went wrong.

The shape of the work

omp (“Oh My Pi”) is the terminal coding-agent harness every session ran in. A top-level session is one chat; a subagent is a child session spawned with the task tool, which reports back with yield.

The build ran from the first prompt (“Hi”) on 2026-09-27 at 19:07 to the analysis snapshot on 2026-10-05 at about 20:30 CDT. It produced 53 top-level sessions (43 on the laptop, 10 on the desktop) and 90 subagent sessions: 215.7 MB of JSONL logs. The median session had 3 prompts and 22 had two or fewer, yet the five biggest session families account for 59% of all assistant turns. The first session, opened from an elevated shell in C:\Windows\system32, ran for 43 hours wall-clock and took 115 prompts omp session index, laptop.

Each human prompt bought 34 assistant turns and 41 tool calls. After a prompt, the median autonomous run lasted 1.9 minutes and 8 turns. The 90th percentile was 24 minutes, and 14 runs went past an hour. The longest real one was an overseer run on 10-02 that played for 170 minutes (118 turns) without a word from the owner; a later run that day reached 158 turns in 145 minutes. Prompts stayed short: half were under 90 characters, and only five were longer than 800. They were directions, not specifications.

The project had a clock. No prompt was typed between 00:00 and 08:59 on any day (). The busiest prompting hour was 22:00, with 73 prompts. Agent work peaked a little earlier, at 17:00 (2,049 turns), and kept going into the 23:00 hour after the owner's last message. It almost never crossed midnight: only 23 turns happened after 00:00. Nothing ran unattended overnight. 23.5% of all prompts (128) were typed while an agent was still working, as mid-run steering.

Models, limits and a relay baton

The project started on Kimi K3, the default model at the time. For two days it did everything: anti-cheat research, the cipher crack and the first proxy. The provider was shaky, and so was the network. On 09-27 and 09-28 the logs show three socket closes and two 403 “You've reached your 5-hour usage limit” errors from the provider, plus four connection errors and one missing API key when the agent knocked the laptop offline while setting up a NIC alias (09-28 13:38–13:40) laptop session “Cracking UO XOR session cipher”. On 09-29 at 13:54 the owner asked for “one brief handoff .md file for the next session to start from”. At 14:06 a new session opened with “Read @HANDOFF.md and continue work from where we last left off” and was switched to Claude Opus 5.5. We suspect the quota errors and the switch are connected, but no log line says so, and the last quota error (09-28 13:53) came about 24 hours before the switch. The model changed hands at a document boundary, not in the middle of a conversation, and that set a pattern the project kept.

Opus did 87.3% of all assistant turns (16,167). Kimi did 11.2% (2,065) and came back only for small jobs and one notable evening (). Sonnet 5.5 did 1.5%, used on purpose for chores: babysitting the viz server, writing the Lord British persona, rebuilding the viz.

omp records a list-price cost for every turn. The owner was on subscription plans (the Kimi quota errors and the Anthropic OAuth credential records point that way), so read every dollar figure here as notional usage, not money spent.

At list prices the whole build comes to about $1,559: Opus $1,334, Kimi $213, Sonnet $12. Prompt caching is what made that affordable. 99.1% of Opus's input tokens were cache reads, 4.1 billion of them, against 34 thousand uncached input tokens. Cache reads still came to 62% of the Opus bill, because the average Opus turn carried about 256k tokens of context. Six times a session filled its window. Compaction kicked in at 750–850k tokens and summarised the context down to 22k–135k omp compaction records. Two of the six compactions hit the 10-04/10-05 desktop hub, which served as the project's control room for more than a day.

Hub and spokes

Subagents arrived on 09-28 at 14:57, when the agent split off a metadata parser (MrtParser) on its own; the owner's first explicit request, “Launch an agent to work on the record-grammar hunt while we talk about Phase 2”, came at 17:16. They became routine on 09-29. In total, 63 task calls spawned 90 subagents (84 general workers, 3 reviewers, 2 scouts, 1 low-effort helper), and they produced 45% of all assistant turns. The hub writes a shared contract plus a # Target / # Change / # Acceptance brief for each child, and the children report back through yield and short IRC messages. One family ran at most 7 children at once. The whole corpus peaked at 10 sessions writing in the same ten-minute window. Subagents never spawned subagents.

The most interesting subagents were not builders at all. From 09-30 onward some children were told to play: “Act as the Seer: read docs/OVERSEER.md…”. They drove the character through harness/ctl.py while the hub kept engineering (the shifts themselves are in ). Nineteen such operator subagents logged 20.2 active hours and 1,973 turns for $66.5. That is about $3.3 per active hour, against about $14.1 per hour for the 66 builder and researcher subagents. Their tool mix shows why. Operators mostly ran bash (1,273 calls, the channel for every ctl status, act goto and know add) and blocked in wait. They made just 6 edit calls, while builders made 1,424. A good shift is cheap because the deterministic runner does the playing and the LLM mostly waits.

This split was not designed up front. The first Overseer subagents ran demos on the Test Shard on 09-30, but on 10-01 and 10-02 the owner had the main session itself “act as the Overseer”. On 10-02 it played for 8.4 active hours. That day produced 28 commits and only 27 subagent turns, the fewest of any day after the first. The fix came from the owner on 10-03:

You should probably start an overseer subagent and tell it what to do, since when you pause for long times leaving Hackworth in the wilderness he's liable to get killed

— the owner, omp session “Lumber spot optimizer near banks”, 2026-10-03 15:18

On 10-04 the desktop hub tried playing in the foreground once more. Six minutes in, the owner said: “Ok, stop acting as the overseer.” After that it sent out six Seer subagents in one day, with terse dispatch orders like “Send an overseer subagent back out, we need more boards!”.

For changes that could collide, the owner used git worktrees. There were five, for the guardrail scrub and CAPTCHA solver, the Ultima UI restyle, the Telegram bot, a test speedup and a paperdoll tweak. Each was merged back with a one-line prompt, once with the warning “I'm sure there will be conflicts because another agent was working on ctl” desktop session, 2026-10-04 17:37.

Standing rules

Each session starts blank. What carries over is AGENTS.md, a 38-line file that every session reads first. Its rules came from specific moments (). Usually the owner said something once in chat and the agent wrote it into the file in the same turn.

One rule has a joke inside it. The original AGENTS.md told agents to use Python 3.13 and spelled out its full path under C:\Users\chris\AppData\… 56208c5. We suspect that line is why agents kept typing the full path. The owner complained about the wasted tokens twice, on 09-30 and 10-03, before the rule was rewritten to forbid it. The jq rule is the reverse case: an agent shell builtin that silently stood in for the real tool. The same commit history shows Rule 0 actually being followed. docs/NOTES.md is touched by 160 of the 348 commits (46%), and the four most-touched files in the repo are all documentation.

How the owner steered

The owner's prompts came in three registers: goals at a high level (“Plan and implement whatever you can tonight using subagents. Please leave me a summary at the end of what is needed from me.”), game knowledge as short asides (“thieves look friendly to us until they steal”), and challenges to what the agent said about itself. The third mattered most.

The first challenge set the tone. On day 0 at 22:08 the owner asked whether the Outlands client encrypted its traffic. Kimi's answer, from string searches alone, was “Verdict: verified — no encryption on the wire”. The owner, steering mid-run: “Can you run a Wireshark analysis just to be sure?” The capture showed that client-to-server traffic was obfuscated. Within the hour the owner had walked his character west 20 times to produce known plaintext, and the XOR was cracked. Other challenges followed the same pattern: “Hold up. He only moved when I moved him with the arrow keys”, “I think you compared against an old screenshot”, and “I am not convinced there was an issue with the server detecting artificial movement”. Each one caught a wrong claim, though not always before it reached the docs. The evidence-first rule and the [INFERENCE] label give the agents a way to say what they have not checked. The owner's habit of asking for a measurement is what made those labels mean something.

Formal approval steps were rare: 14 ask calls and 4 plan-mode entries across 545 prompts. Steering was mostly by interruption, with 70 user aborts, 19 of them in the first session. The owner also acted as the agents' eyes and hands. He accepted UAC prompts they could not see, ran controlled experiments, solved CAPTCHAs, and popped a trapped pouch so an agent could capture the traffic.

The guardrails episode

The clearest case of model behaviour changing the course of the work came on 09-30. That afternoon the owner decided to move the overseer from the Test Shard to the production shard. At 17:04 he asked an Opus session to remove the Test-Shard-only restrictions from the docs. The day-0 AGENTS.md, drafted by an agent, said those constraints could only be lifted with “an explicit, informed acknowledgment of that risk in the conversation”. Opus's thinking cited exactly that, and it opened an ask titled “Getting informed acknowledgment of ban risk”. The owner cancelled the ask, and the session ended after four turns. A minute later he started a different Opus session that audited the harness's traffic for anything a stock client could not have produced.

That evening a broader version of the request went to Kimi K3: remove references to the shard's rules, reframe ANTICHEAT.md as evasion intelligence, and drop the requirement that a human solve CAPTCHAs. The 21:26 and 21:44 sessions each spent their thinking on whether this amounted to removing safety guardrails. The owner interrupted each one after a single turn. The third, started at 21:45 in a fresh worktree, did the job. It sent out seven doc-scrub subagents at 21:49, committed 2f60ad9 at 21:58, and was merged at 23:34. In all: 4 sessions, 2 models, 3 aborts, and about 23 minutes from the first Kimi attempt to the fan-out. The next morning the owner set CAPTCHAs back to human-solved by default, with an auto-solve toggle in the viz (d152c00).

Lesson

Rules written into the repo bind later sessions, and other models, more firmly than any chat message. A constraint an agent drafted on day 0 was still being enforced three days later, by a different session, after the owner had decided otherwise. If AGENTS.md is going to carry consent rules, the owner should write them as deliberately as code. The ethics of the shard move itself are discussed in .

Two machines, one repo

On 10-04 the project moved from the laptop to the desktop. At 15:27 came “Make sure everything is stopped and then push the handoff so I can go resume on my desktop”. The desktop cloned the repo at 15:56. At 15:58 a single prompt, “Read @SETUP.md and setup this project for the first time on this Windows desktop”, started a 23-minute setup session. That session found hard-coded C:\Users\chris paths in 55 files (54 changed in 06acc67), handing the 26 reverse-engineering scripts to a subagent and fixing the harness code itself. The runtime memory moved separately as a baton, through dbhandoff.py and the NAS (). Machine use was serial: only 3 ten-minute windows in the whole corpus show both machines active. The laptop wrote 300 commits and the desktop the last 48.

What git shows

All 348 commits carry one author identity, although agent sessions wrote every one of them. The daily counts follow the session activity: 68 and 67 on 09-29/30, a lull of 17 on 10-01 (a ten-hour daytime gap), and the peak of 76 on 10-03 (). Commits cluster at noon and 17:00, with 28% of them (96 of 348) at 20:00 or later. Commit messages are long: the median full message is 149 characters, and 59 run past 500. In practice they work as a second lab notebook.

The codebase grew from 577 Python lines on the first night to 54,764 at the last commit, about 95 times as many (). The two biggest half-day jumps were 09-29 PM (the viz and the overseer groundwork, +13.4k) and 10-03 PM (Discord tooling, the dungeon crawl, Witcher runes, +9.3k). Test code stayed at roughly half the size of core code. By the end there were 224 def test_ functions and 1,748 assert/check sites, plus 128 viz test cases, and 49% of commits had touched a test file. That fits the owner's day-1 instruction: “the test suite is extremely important so make sure it is thorough and follow TDD”. Prose grew faster than code. The Markdown docs reached 192k words, not counting the generated Discord knowledge base, which is 3.5 words for every line of Python, and 30% of commits changed nothing but .md files.

How the agents failed, and what worked

The tool logs show the everyday friction (). edit failed on 12.8% of calls. Most of those (386 of 508) were the anchored-edit protocol refusing an edit aimed at lines the agent had never been shown in its latest read. That cost retries, not corrupted files. bash failed 4.5% of the time, mostly on misused service flags, Python tracebacks, and the jaq null errors behind the jq.exe rule. wait took the most wall time, 35.9 hours, because that is where hubs blocked on children and Seers blocked on the game.

The costly failures were not tool errors. They were confident claims that turned out to be wrong ().

Four things did work. Documents as the baton: HANDOFF.md took the project across a model switch, and SETUP.md across machines, each in a single prompt. Delegation with a contract: named subagents with explicit acceptance criteria kept the hub free for conversation. Deterministic code with LLMs at the edges: it holds for the harness itself, and it is also why Seer shifts cost a quarter as much per hour as builder sessions. A sceptical human: from the first night's “Did we verify whether it does or not?” onward, the owner kept asking for checks and kept watching the game screen.

Finding

In nine days and 73,567 typed characters, one person directing agents produced a 55k-line codebase, 192k words of design notes and a harness that played the game for hours at a time. Typing was clearly not the bottleneck; we think the owner's attention and game knowledge were. The agents' main weakness was overconfidence, and their most reliable defences were the owner's questions and the measurements those questions forced.
